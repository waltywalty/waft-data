Sweep Entries and the Aussie Filter

The sweep entry gets you a much better fill and still loses — because the days that sweep are the days the bias was wrong. But the AUD question turned up the first thing in this study that survives every test I could throw at it.

1,212 gold trades 16 sweep variants 7 session exits 2 AUDUSD feeds, both UTC-verified
Verdict

The sweep-and-reclaim entry does not work — best of sixteen variants is a 1.096 profit factor (60-minute range, low of the breakout candle), and most are far below 1. The reason is sharp: requiring a sweep selects, after the fact, for the days the bias failed.

The AUD idea works, but not in the form you posed it. Whether AUDUSD moves with gold on the day tells you nothing (p = 0.20). Whether gold and AUDUSD have been coupled over the past month tells you a great deal: filtering to days when the 20-day correlation is at or below 0.5 lifts the profit factor from 1.128 to 1.337 and the t-statistic from 1.14 to 2.76. The excluded days run at 0.848.

01The sweep entry, tested

Your specification, implemented literally: establish the bias from the opening-range breakout, wait for a counter-directional sweep of a liquidity level, require a reclaim, then enter in the bias direction.

Four definitions of "liquidity level" were tested, because the phrase carries real ambiguity: the most recent confirmed swing low/high (a 5-bar fractal, updated as the day progresses); the low of the breakout candle; the Asia session extreme; and the far side of the opening range. The reclaim requires a close back on the correct side within 1–6 five-minute bars. Two guards keep the setup honest — the sweep may not exceed the opposite edge of the opening range (which would invalidate the bias outright), and the swept level must sit on the far side of the breakout price, so the entry is a genuine pullback rather than a chase.

A profit factor of 1.93 on the days price never comes back, against 0.61 on the days it does. The sweep is not a setup — it is a symptom. On gold, price returning to take out a swing level after the Asia breakout is the market telling you the move has no sponsorship. Waiting for it means systematically trading only the days that fail.

This also explains the earlier pullback-entry result: every mechanism that requires price to come back to you is buying adverse selection.

One mechanical note. Putting the stop at the sweep extreme, which the pattern seems to invite, is unusable: the reclaim close sits only a few dollars above the swept low, so 91% of trades are stopped out and the win rate collapses to 8%. Any live version needs a buffer of at least half the range width below the sweep.

Same-day agreement: nothing

Taking your question at face value first — does AUDUSD moving in the same direction as gold during the Asia session confirm the bias? Over 362 days where both feeds exist, agreement gives a 0.937 profit factor and divergence 1.369, with t = -1.28, p = 0.20. Directionally it hints that divergence is better, but on this sample that is not a finding.

04The regime, not the day

The daily series runs the full five years, so the coupling regime can be measured where same-day co-movement could not. This is where it gets interesting.

Finding

Take the 20-day rolling correlation between gold and AUDUSD daily returns, computed only from data available before the session opens. Trade the opening-range breakout only when that correlation is at or below 0.5.

1212 trades → 747. Profit factor 1.128 → 1.337. Expectancy $+0.82 → $+2.01 per trade. t-statistic 1.14 → 2.76. The 465 excluded days run at a 0.848 profit factor and $-1.08 per trade — they are where the losses live.

Why it might be real

A high gold–AUD correlation means gold is trading as a dollar and risk-sentiment proxy, moving with the same macro flow that drives every other USD pair. A low or negative correlation means gold is being driven by something of its own — central bank buying, safe-haven demand, a metals-specific bid. The result says intraday breakouts continue when gold has its own sponsor and fail when it is just expressing the dollar. That is a mechanism, not just a correlation, which is the minimum bar for taking a filter seriously.

And the rest of the battery

  • Continuous, no threshold at all. Spearman correlation between the 20-day coupling and the trade's return is -0.080 (p = 0.0054); the regression slope is -33 basis points per unit of correlation (p = 0.0014). The effect does not depend on where the cut is placed.
  • Not a calendar proxy. The share of high-correlation days ranges from 18% to 58% across years and does not line up with the good and bad years. Within-year, low-correlation days beat high-correlation days in 4 of the 5 years with enough data.
  • Not volatility or trendiness in disguise. The 20-day coupling correlates +0.03 with realised volatility and −0.08 with a trendiness measure. Double-sorted, the effect survives inside every volatility tercile and every trendiness tercile.
  • Block-shuffle placebo: p < 0.001. Shuffling the regime labels in blocks — preserving how persistent the regime is, destroying only which days carry which label — beat the observed edge in 0 of 5,000 draws.
  • Randomisation on direction: p = 0.0002. Keeping the filtered entry and exit times but choosing the side at random.
  • Independent re-implementation. The headline was reproduced by a second engine written from scratch, not sharing code with the first, to rule out a bug.

06The rule, stated plainly

  1. Each day before 09:30 HKT, compute the 20-day rolling correlation between gold and AUDUSD daily returns, using data through yesterday's close.
  2. If that correlation is above 0.5, do not trade. That is roughly 38% of days.
  3. Otherwise form the opening range from 09:30 HKT — 60 minutes tested hardest, but 15 and 30 also improve.
  4. Enter on the first candle to close beyond the range, in that direction.
  5. Hold to the New York close. Later exits beat earlier ones consistently; exiting at or before the London open remains the worst choice in every test.
  6. No sweep entry, no pullback, no confirmation beyond the breakout close.

07What would still make me wrong

  • The underlying strategy is still weak. Unfiltered it is a 1.128 profit factor. The filter is doing all the work, which means the whole thing rests on one variable holding up.
  • One instrument, one regime variable, five years. The honest test is whether the same conditioning works on silver, on the London-open range, and on the twelve months since the data ends in August 2025.
  • I found this after a large search. The placebo, the monotonic threshold, the generalisation across twelve configurations and the out-of-sample consistency are what raise it above the earlier false positives — but a t-statistic of 2.76 on 747 trades is a finding that deserves forward testing, not size.
  • 2023 was negative. A year of losses inside a validated rule is normal, and also exactly what an over-fitted rule looks like early in its decay.
  • Paper-trade the filter for a quarter before it changes any position sizing. The cheapest next test is to compute the correlation live and simply log which days it would have skipped.
