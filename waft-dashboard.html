How this is measured, and the three things it refuses to tell you

What is counted. Every five-minute bar in the relay’s archive is dropped into the half hour it belongs to. Travel sums the bars’ own high-lows, which is additive and therefore partitions a day exactly — within any single day the buckets sum to 100%. The figure published per bucket is the median across days, and medians do not add: the middle day of one half hour is rarely the middle day of the next, so the column you see sums to roughly 108%, not 100. Nothing on this card quotes that sum as a whole; where a run of buckets is reported as a share — “so much travel still ahead” — it is divided by the column’s own total rather than by 100. Range is high minus low over a span, which is not additive either: a session can travel enormously and finish where it started, and that gap is the entire subject of the range÷travel column. Volume is the same bars’ volume, as a share of the day. Nothing here is a price, so nothing here goes stale when the market moves; it goes stale when the archive grows, and the header on the block carries the window it was measured over.

Which days count, and it is two different answers. The bucket table needs a day whose total travel is a day’s travel, so a day-label is measured only if it traded all three sessions. That test is not cosmetic: the archive’s day begins at the New York cash close, which peels the hour of trade after a Friday off into a label of its own — twelve bars, two buckets, and inside that label those two buckets are half the day’s travel each and 100% of its range. The day-range median needs something stricter still, because the projection above divides by it: only a finished day has a whole day’s range, and the newest label in the archive is the one being published into right now. So the buckets are measured on 5 days and the day-range median on 5, and the two numbers are different on purpose.

The cumulative curve is not guaranteed to rise. Each figure is the median of that bucket over the days that reached it, and different buckets were reached by different subsets of days, so two neighbours can be measured on different evidence. That is why the third panel is columns rather than a line — a line would draw an interpolation between two numbers that were never measured together — and why every bar carries its own day count on hover and in the table.

Three things this refuses to publish. First, any day-of-week or month-of-year statistic. The archive holds 5 trading days, which is one observation of each weekday and none of any month; a weekday table built on that would be five numbers with an n of one apiece, and the fact that it would look exactly like the tables above is what makes it dangerous. It becomes measurable at 20 trading days — about 26 Aug 2026. Second, any direction rate: 4 of the 5 days closed up, so up-day share, close-above-open and high-made-in-session are all that one fact wearing different hats. Magnitude does not have that problem, which is why this card measures magnitude. Third, a continuation-versus-reversal rate — and this card could have produced one by a back door, from the first New York sweep of the London range. It measured ES continuing 2 of 5 and RTY 4 of 5. Those disagree with each other, are one sample of five apiece, and would have sat on the same page as a killzone card that says in its own words that the direction question is not yet answerable. One page does not get two answers to one question depending on which card you read.

Two contracts, and only two. The relay banks five-minute bars for ES and RTY. Cross-market agreement

Why that is not an edge. Divergent swings did turn a little more often than a random row, and so did confirmed ones — which is the tell: most of the gap belongs to being at a swing at all, not to diverging. The two bases are also not mirror images. Over this window a live row rose 62% of the time and fell 37%, because the window trended; reading the high rows against the rising base would manufacture a large edge out of nothing but the drift.

And the n are smaller than they look. Each swing is counted once per usable pair, so one ES high that diverged against three partners lands in that column three times — no honest standard error can be computed from it. The saved run also prints a p-value; it is an exact binomial against a fair coin, which is not the question this card asks, so it is not quoted here.

Why HSI has no board

Over the same 160 hours the relay published 1465 HSI quotes carrying 207 price changes, and 86% of consecutive quotes are a repeat. That number is not about the feed. 85% of those samples were taken while the Hong Kong exchange was shut, and across the shut hours the repeat rate is 99% because there is nothing for the quote to do. Inside the weekday cash session it is 14%, and with the 12:00–13:00 lunch halt taken out, 5%. During the hours it trades, this quote moves on 95 of every 100 consecutive samples. It is a live tape.

The longest unbroken run of identical quotes in the archive is 46h — and it runs Saturday lunchtime to Monday morning, so it is the weekend, not a stall. Restricted to hours the exchange was open, the longest run is 54m, and that one straddles the lunch halt; take the halt out and the longest the price ever stood still while Hong Kong was trading is 12m.

So the board is refused — but not for the reason this card used to give. It is refused because 4 cash sessions have yielded 223 in-session samples between them, roughly 56 a day at one every 6 minutes, and because the feed carries no volume at all. What to do with the dollar todayDo not trade the direction. Against ES the dollar reads −0.18 over the whole window and −0.08 over its second half — the tie is real, inverse and halving. Three other cards on this page use the dollar to explain an equity move; on this window that explanation carries about 3% of the variance, and less than that lately. Trade the size instead. On an ordinary half hour ES rose 61% of the time and moved 0.048% in absolute terms. In the half hour after the dollar’s largest moves — the top decile, anything past 0.085% in thirty minutes — it rose 47% of the time and moved 0.071%. The direction edge vanishes and the range grows by about 47%. It does not matter which way the dollar went: after a fall ES rose 45% of the time, after a rise 50%. So a violent dollar is a volatility signal and specifically not a direction one — widen stops, expect the next half hour to be bigger, and take the direction from something else. The one dependable pair is gold, at −0.40 and holding across both halves. It is also contemporaneous: gold is a mirror the dollar is already in, not a window onto the next half hour.
